---

## The Short Answer

Pancreatic cancer that has spread to the abdominal lining (peritoneum) remains one of the hardest cancers to treat. As of mid-2026, **no vaccine trial has specifically studied peritoneal metastasis patients** — meaning there is no direct clinical evidence that cancer vaccines work in this exact situation.

But the research is moving fast, and the picture is more nuanced than a simple "no." Here's what you actually need to know.

---

## Why Peritoneal Metastasis Is Especially Hard

When pancreatic cancer spreads to the peritoneum, it does something most other metastases don't — it coats the abdominal wall and organs with tiny tumor seeds, often producing fluid (ascites) that causes bloating and pain. Surgery becomes impossible. Standard chemotherapy struggles to penetrate the abdominal lining effectively.

The prognosis is stark. Without effective treatment, median survival is roughly 3–6 months. Even with aggressive chemotherapy, median survival barely stretches to 9–11 months. Five-year survival for metastatic pancreatic cancer sits at just **3.2%**.

This is why researchers have been racing to find alternatives — including cancer vaccines.

---

## What Is a Cancer Vaccine, Anyway?

Unlike a flu shot that *prevents* disease, a therapeutic cancer vaccine tries to *train the immune system to recognize and attack existing tumors*. The idea is elegant: identify proteins unique to the tumor, present them to the immune system, and let T-cells do the killing.

Pancreatic cancer has traditionally been called a "cold" tumor — meaning it doesn't attract immune attention naturally. That's what makes vaccine development so challenging.

---

## The Four Vaccine Approaches Currently Underway

**1. Personalized mRNA neoantigen vaccines** — The most cutting-edge approach. A tumor biopsy is sequenced, unique mutations are identified, and a custom mRNA vaccine is built for that specific patient. BioNTech's BNT122 is the flagship example. In an early trial of post-surgical patients, half the patients mounted a strong immune response, and those responders showed a remarkable 87.5% survival rate at 4–6 years. But this trial only included *post-surgery* patients, not those with metastasis.

**2. Multi-peptide vaccines (OSE2101/Tedopi)** — A "ready-to-use" approach targeting five common cancer-associated proteins. This is the only vaccine to complete a Phase 2 trial *in advanced/metastatic* pancreatic cancer patients. The results were sobering: median survival was 15.5 months with vaccine + chemotherapy, versus 17.3 months with chemotherapy alone — essentially no difference. The vaccine did not outperform standard treatment in this trial.

**3. Whole-cell vaccines (GVAX)** — Using genetically modified pancreatic cancer cells to spark an immune response. A Phase 2 trial combining GVAX with immunotherapy and radiation in locally advanced patients reported a median survival of 21.8 months — but these were *pre-metastatic* patients who had not yet developed distant spread.

**4. KRAS-targeted vaccines** — Since ~90% of pancreatic cancers carry a KRAS mutation, targeting this protein is attractive. Johns Hopkins developed a peptide vaccine covering six common KRAS subtypes. In a Phase 1 trial of post-surgical patients, 91.7% mounted an immune response, and responders showed significantly longer disease-free survival. But again, no metastatic patients were included.

## What the Data Actually Shows: A Survival Comparison

| Treatment | Setting | Median Survival |
|---|---|---|
| **BNT122 mRNA vaccine** (responders) | Post-surgical | >48 months |
| **KRAS peptide vaccine** | Post-surgical | 29.6 months |
| **GVAX + immunotherapy** | Locally advanced | 21.8 months |
| **OSE2101 + FOLFIRI** | Advanced/metastatic | 15.5 months |
| **FOLFIRI alone** | Advanced/metastatic | 17.3 months |
| **NALIRIFOX** (standard chemo) | Advanced/metastatic | 11.1 months |
| **AG regimen** (standard chemo) | Advanced/metastatic | 9.2 months |

The headline finding: **vaccine results in early-stage patients look dramatically better than anything available for late-stage patients — but we cannot yet say whether vaccines work for patients who have already metastasized.** The best-performing vaccine in a metastatic trial (OSE2101) was *inferior to* standard chemotherapy alone in the same trial.

---

## Is There Any Hope for Peritoneal Metastasis Patients?

Yes — but it's important to be realistic about what "hope" means right now:

**The combination strategy is the most promising path forward.** Instead of asking "does a vaccine work alone?", researchers are now asking whether combining a KRAS inhibitor (which shrinks tumors quickly) with a vaccine (which trains long-term immune surveillance) could produce synergistic results. A RAS inhibitor called daraxonrasib recently doubled median survival in a Phase 3 trial for RAS-driven metastatic pancreatic cancer — opening the door to combination approaches.

**Timing is everything.** Vaccines take time to work. An mRNA vaccine can take weeks to manufacture after a biopsy. Immune responses can take months to translate into clinical results. A patient whose disease is rapidly progressing may lose eligibility for a trial before the vaccine even has a chance to work. Families who want to explore vaccine options should start the conversation *while the disease is still stable* — not as a last resort.

**Clinical trials are the only way to access these therapies right now.** No cancer vaccine is approved for any indication in pancreatic cancer. Every patient currently receiving a vaccine is doing so through a clinical trial.

---

## What Patients and Families Should Actually Do Today

Standard chemotherapy is still the backbone of treatment for peritoneal metastasis, and should not be abandoned without a plan. For patients in good physical condition, NALIRIFOX and FOLFIRINOX are the best-evidenced options.

For those interested in exploring vaccines, the practical steps are:

1. **Get comprehensive molecular testing** — BRCA, KRAS subtype, MSI/MMR, HLA typing, and tumor mutation burden. This determines what trials are even accessible.

2. **Consider trial consultation before it's too late** — As soon as first-line treatment stabilizes the disease, start talking to research centers about vaccine options. Waiting until third- or fourth-line failure often means losing eligibility due to declining performance status.

3. **Prioritize centers actively running relevant trials:**
- **Ruijin Hospital (Shanghai)** — mRNA-0217/S001 personalized neoantigen vaccine
- **Peking University Cancer Hospital (Beijing)** — EVM16 personalized mRNA vaccine (IIT study)
- **Fudan University Cancer Hospital (Shanghai)** — RGL-270 neoantigen vaccine
- **Changhai Hospital (Shanghai)** — CAR-NK cell therapy

4. **Don't abandon standard care** — The ideal approach is using chemotherapy to buy time and control disease, while in parallel exploring whether a vaccine trial or combination strategy is available.

---

## The Bottom Line

Cancer vaccines for pancreatic cancer are genuinely promising — especially the personalized mRNA approach pioneered by BioNTech and now being replicated by Chinese biotechs like Everest Medicines. The early post-surgical results are, quite frankly, extraordinary.

But for patients who already have peritoneal metastasis, **there is no vaccine trial data specific to their situation, and no vaccine is proven to outperform chemotherapy in this population**. The path forward is through combination trials and careful timing — starting the conversation early, not as a final act.

The science is advancing. In the next few years, as combination strategies mature and larger trials report results, that picture may change. For now, the best strategy is: keep standard treatment as your foundation, and explore vaccine trials aggressively but early.
